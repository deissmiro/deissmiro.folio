Homepage and Search Redesign

One home screen for every trip. Interest in hotels grew by 9.3%, and new users booked buses 6.7% more often.

Role
Senior Product Designer
Company
Tutu
Platforms
iOS, Android, web
When
May – Sep 2026
BeforeAfter

Problem

Tutu sells flights, trains, buses, commuter trains, stays, car rental and tours. The home screen decides which of them people ever see, and the old one did a poor job of it.

The goal was to refresh the look of the home screen, search and verticals. The current mobile home screen:

  • was visually overloaded and fragmented
  • used an outdated visual language for icons and communication design
  • scaled poorly for promo campaigns
  • created a lot of cognitive noise before the search even started
  • didn’t feel like a modern travel super-app

An audit of the screen showed where exactly this happened:

Benchmarking

With the task framed and the audit showing what was wrong with the current home screen, I went to see how others do it: travel services, and also super-apps, delivery and marketplaces.

What I paid attention to

  • How services are shown on the home screen: icons, tiles or cards
  • Where search lives and how many taps it takes to start
  • How promo fits in without competing with search
  • How they set priorities: which services get more space and which go further down
  • Whether all services fit on the first screen or hide behind scrolling and a “More” button
  • How illustrations and labels help tell one service from another at a glance
  • How they bring people back: recent searches, saved trips, personal picks
  • How the same home screen works in the app and on the web

Hypotheses

01Tiles sized by priority. Large tiles will lead people into products beyond the ticket they came for.
02Every vertical on one screen. Without scrolling, car rental, tours and commuter trains will finally get visits.
03One search field. A single field will free the screen and still be the obvious place to start.
04Promo inside the screen. Built-in promo will get more attention than a banner, without competing with search.
05Picture plus label. 3D illustrations will make verticals faster to recognise than small icons.
06One structure for app and web. People moving between them will find things where they expect.

Research

User testing

I tested the drafts with users in three rounds. People got tasks, chose where to start a search and compared the new home screen with the current one.

All icons are in quick access Picture plus label stand out, everything is clear right away The interface isn’t overloaded

The final round had 100 people from a research panel, most of them new to Tutu:

  • 97–100% found it easy to start a search for flights, stays and buses
  • 94% were sure they picked the right place to start
  • 73% chose the new home screen over the current one

Heatmaps

The heatmaps showed where attention went. The largest tile got the most taps, and new users found the smaller verticals once they were visible without scrolling. That made size and position my main tools for moving people into other products.

Design

With the audit, the hypotheses and three rounds of testing in hand, I chose the layout that tested best and built it for the app and the web as one system. Here is what changed.

Verticals as tiles, sized by priority

Stays got the biggest tile, Flights and Rail sit next to it, and the rest fit into one row of icons that no longer scrolls. Every vertical is on the first screen, and the size of a tile says how much we want people to try it.

All icons are in quick access

One search entry

The heavy form collapsed into one field, “Find tickets, stays or tours”. It opens a single search screen with Tickets, Stays, Car and Tours on top, transport modes below and the familiar From, To, When and Who fields.

Picture plus label stand out, everything is clear right away

Promo inside the flow

The banner that competed with search moved into the hero, and offers like the “Good deal” carousel got their own place in the feed. A new visual language ties it together: 3D soft-illustration icons, an updated color system and a calmer rhythm of cards.

The interface isn’t overloaded

Every step of the search changed: the form itself, the city picker, the calendar and the passenger picker.

Every case and state

Behind the final screens is a full spec in Figma. For the home page I covered breakpoints from mobile to desktop, logged-in and guest users, the cashback badge and favorites, every vertical and header alternatives. For search: the current flow, light and dark themes, transition logic, history, field behavior and validation, passengers, calendar and dates, and the components behind it all.

Results

The A/B test ran for two weeks. Interest in hotels from the home screen grew by 9.3%, new users ordered buses 6.7% more often, and the verticals that used to hide at the end of the row came alive: taps into Car grew by 31% and into Tours by 13%.

Kite Glyphs: a Figma Plugin for Font Icons

How I automated work with font icons and saved product teams’ time: inserting an icon went from up to five actions to one click.

Role
Product Designer, design and build
Company
Tutu
Product
Figma plugin
Stack
JS/TS, Figma API

Problem

Tutu uses its own icon font, so designers can put icons right into text. In practice, working with it turned into routine and a source of mistakes.

Main pain points

01Too many clicks. Inserting a single icon took up to five actions.
02Lost time. Finding the right glyph in a huge shared library or a random file was close to impossible.
03No documentation. Nobody wanted to describe the complicated process in writing, let alone read it.

How it worked before

Once
  1. Download the font
  2. Open the icon library or file
For every icon
  1. Find the icon
  2. Copy its code or name
  3. Paste it into a text layer
  4. Detach the text style
  5. Switch the font from Tutu Sans to Icons

Research

The work started with research: what already exists, what we actually need, and whether we need a tool at all.

  • How designers work today. I looked at how designers use the font now and what third-party solutions are on the market.
  • Figma API. I studied the platform’s limits to understand how to connect the plugin interface to the Figma canvas.
  • MVP requirements. From both, I put together what the first version had to do.

Solution

I designed and built a plugin that removed the routine completely.

Instant search

A grid of every icon, grouped by category, with search by name in English or Russian right inside Figma.

Insert in one click

A designer just clicks an icon, and it lands on the canvas as a text layer already set in the icon font.

Results

5 → 1Actions to insert an icon
0Manual font and style switching
1Place to find every glyph

Kite Design System: Rebuilding the Color Architecture

Kite serves four brands at once: tutu, alfa, rasp and yandex. I rebuilt its color token system from the palette up: a flat structure without contrast and content modes, a token name with clear rules, and one source of truth for every color.

Company
Tutu travel
Role
Senior Product Designer
Scope
Design system, color tokens
Year
2026

Introduction

Kite is the design system behind Tutu’s products. It serves four brands at the same time, so every color decision is multiplied by four.

The color system grew around one brand. When the product needed a redesign and new brands and themes, it stopped scaling. I took on the whole color layer: audit, new architecture, new naming and migration.

Goal

Rebuild the color architecture so it scales, supports several brands and makes the product easier to grow.

  • Audit the existing palette and tokens
  • Collect requirements from business, marketing and product
  • Design a new architecture of color tokens
  • Prepare the system for several brands
  • Update Kite components and write documentation and a migration plan

Problem

The audit found problems on all four levels: theme, palette, semantics and components.

One name, four brands

The palette used one set of names for all brands: strawberry, banana, kiwi, teal, rose, halva. The names were invented for one brand. In the alfa or rasp mode the same name pointed to a completely different color. Adding a brand meant stretching its colors over someone else’s names and explaining to the team why banana isn’t yellow.

aubergine
tutu
alfa
rasp
yandex

The palette stored the brand, not colors

Switching the palette mode only swapped hex values. Everything else that makes a brand stayed the same for all four:

  • spacing, padding and gaps
  • sizes of buttons, icons and inputs
  • corner radius
  • typography
  • shadows, elevation and blur
  • density: compact and comfortable

Hardcoded semantics

The palette held only solid hex values without alpha. Transparent colors lived elsewhere, with their hex written in by hand. When a brand color changed, the palette updated and the transparent tokens didn’t. There was no single source of truth. On top of that, semantic tokens were named after colors, like rose or teal, instead of what they mean.

Too many modes

Color was spread over five collections, each with its own modes:

CollectionTokensModes
theme862light / dark
palette709tutu / alfa / rasp / yandex
contrast128low / high
content36on low / on high
state6default / hover / active / focus

Hypotheses

A flat structure with clear naming will:

  • turn adding a brand into mapping values, not renaming the palette
  • give every color one source of truth, transparent ones included
  • let designers and developers pick a token by its meaning, without guessing
  • make support simpler and new features faster to ship

Research

Palette audit

I went through every color and token in Kite level by level: theme, palette, semantics and components.

Requirements

I collected what business, marketing and product need from color: brands, themes, promo and status messages.

Shared language

I wrote down the base concepts so the team talks about color the same way.

Theming

The interface changes its look in a second without changing the structure of the screen.

Palette

The full set of paints we have. Raw building material.

Semantics

The level of meaning and rules: which role a paint plays in the interface.

Component tokens

Fine settings for specific parts of the interface. The deepest level.

Design

A flat structure

I removed the contrast and content modes (on high / on low). Contrast and the content that sits on a surface are now part of the token itself, so a token doesn’t change its meaning depending on a hidden mode.

CollectionModes
themelight / darkkept
palettetutu / alfa / rasp / yandexkept
contrastlow / highremoved
contenton low / on highremoved
statedefault / hover / active / focuskept

How a token name works

Every color token is built from the same parts. The first ones are constant and say what the color is. The last ones are modifiers and pick the exact variant.

contentGroupWhere the color is used: background, overlay, content, border
accentMeaningIts role: accent, status, neutral, brand, static
unoColor schemeA variant inside the meaning: uno…penta, positive, warning, negative, info
primaryHierarchyHow important it is: primary, secondary, tertiary. Surfaces use contrast instead: low, med, high

A new palette

Ten color ramps with fourteen steps each, from 100 to 950. Hover a swatch to see its token, click to copy the hex.

aubergine-550 · #6618E0

Results

−2mode collections: contrast and content are gone
4brands on one architecture
140colors in 10 ramps of the new palette

What got simpler

BeforeNow
Picking a tokenFind the token, then check which contrast and content mode it sits inRead the name: group, meaning, scheme, hierarchy. No hidden modes
Adding a brandStretch its colors over another brand’s namesMap the brand’s paints to the same meanings
Changing a brand colorUpdate the palette, then hunt down hand-written hex in transparent tokensChange it in one place, transparent colors follow
NamingColors named by how they look: rose, tealColors named by what they do. Teal and rose are deprecated
Talking about colorEveryone used their own wordsOne vocabulary for designers and developers, written down in the docs

The base layer is done. Next come info color, the medium contrast and brand modes on the same architecture.

Conclusions

  • A palette should describe paints, not a brand. Brand lives in the mapping.
  • Modes hide meaning. A flat token with a readable name is easier to use than a token that changes behind a switch.
  • One source of truth for every color, including transparent ones, removes a whole class of bugs.
  • Shared vocabulary matters as much as the tokens: the team now talks about group, meaning, scheme and hierarchy.
